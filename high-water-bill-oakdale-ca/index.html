High Water Bill in Oakdale, CA

A high water bill is usually caused by a leak, a running toilet, irrigation or a change in how much water you use. Read the meter with everything off, test the toilets with dye and check the sprinklers, and you will find the cause in most cases.

This guide gives a step by step method and explains when a hidden leak is the likely cause and who should find it.

Step one, read the meter

  1. Turn everything off

    Stop the sprinklers, the ice maker and appliances that use water.

  2. Find the meter

    Open the meter box, usually near the street, and lift the lid.

  3. Look at the leak indicator

    Many meters have a small triangle or wheel that spins with any flow.

  4. Check again after fifteen minutes

    If the numbers moved, water is going somewhere.

If the meter does not move, the extra use may be from a change in habits or from a billing reading issue, and you can ask the city to review the reading.

Why summer bills run high in the valley

Summer in the valley is hot and dry, and the historic record near Oakdale shows July highs averaging in the low 100s. Lawns, trees and pools need more water, and irrigation timers may run longer in July than in April.

A bill that jumps in summer is often the sprinklers. A broken head or a stuck valve can flood a zone for hours. Run each zone one at a time and watch for pooling, geysers and soggy ground. If you are unsure how the system is set, ask your landscaper to review the schedule.

Toilets are the silent offenders

A toilet with a leaking flapper can lose a large amount of water each day with no sound. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank, do not flush and wait. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper is leaking.

A replacement flapper is inexpensive, and our toilet repair crew can fit one in minutes. Check every toilet in the house, since one failing flapper is rarely the only one.

When a hidden leak is likely

If the meter moves with everything off and the house shut off at the main does not stop it, the leak is on the service line. If the meter stops when the main is closed, the leak is in the house.

We find hidden leaks with pressure tests, acoustic listening tools and thermal cameras, and we can check the line out to the meter. Ask your city about any adjustment policy for leaks, since many utilities consider them after a documented repair.

Pools, lawns and outdoor use

Pools lose water to evaporation, and a pool that needs frequent topping up in summer adds to the bill. A float valve or an auto fill that sticks can waste a lot of water without a visible leak.

Lawns suffer too, since overwatering is a common habit. If your lawn has dry and soggy spots at once, the sprinkler coverage is uneven. A short irrigation tune up pays for itself in a season.

Reading the bill itself

Compare this bill to the same month last year, not to last month. Seasonal use changes a lot, and a fair comparison helps you decide whether the number is a real jump.

Look at the days in the billing period and the meter readings printed on the bill. If you spot an estimated reading, ask the city for an actual reading, since estimates can swing in either direction.

Hot water and the bill

Water heating adds to the energy bill rather than the water bill, but a leaking hot line shows up on both. A hot water leak is a double cost, so the heater runs more and the meter moves.

If your gas or electric bill also jumped, mention it when you call. It points us toward the hot side of the system when we start testing.

A short checklist for the month

  • Read the meter at the same time each day for a week.
  • Test every toilet with dye.
  • Run each sprinkler zone and watch for pooling.
  • Look under sinks for damp cabinets and green crust at fittings.

Next steps

  • Photograph the meter reading and the date.
  • Keep your repair receipts and any plumber report.
  • Call the city billing office about the reading and any leak policy.
  • Fix the leak quickly, so the next bill returns to normal.

Pressure, regulators and the bill

The California Plumbing Code, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), caps building water pressure at 80 psi with a regulator above that. A failing regulator can raise pressure and make small leaks run faster, which shows up on the bill.
