Sewer Smell in the House in Oakdale, CA

A sewer smell inside the house usually comes from a dry drain trap, a loose toilet seal, a blocked vent or a damaged sewer line. Start with the drain you use least, since a trap that has dried out lets gas rise.

This guide lists the causes from the simplest to the most serious, with safe checks and clear signs that mean you should call a plumber.

Start with the cheap, simple checks. Many smells are solved in a minute by running water in a drain.

Safe checks to do first

  1. Find where it is strongest

    Walk through the house and note the room and the fixture closest to the smell.

  2. Run water in unused drains

    Pour a quart of water into floor drains, guest tubs and rarely used sinks, then add a spoon of cooking oil to slow evaporation.

  3. Check the toilet base

    Look for water, rocking or stains around the toilet.

  4. Listen for gurgling

    Gurgles after you drain a fixture point to a vent or a partial clog.

If the smell is strong, you feel dizzy or you smell rotten eggs along with a hissing sound, leave and call the gas utility. A gas leak is not a plumbing problem, and it is dangerous.

Why dry traps are common in the valley

The historic climate record near Oakdale shows hot summers with July highs averaging in the low 100s. Heat and dry indoor air let water in a rarely used trap evaporate faster, so a guest bath or a garage floor drain can go dry in a few weeks.

Many valley homes have floor drains in garages and laundry rooms. If you see no water in the drain and a smell comes up, pour water into it and the smell will usually stop. A trap primer, a small device that refills the trap, is a lasting fix for a floor drain that dries out.

Vents and why they matter

Every drain needs a vent so air can enter behind the water. A blocked vent pulls water out of traps and lets gas into the room, and it makes drains gurgle.

Vent blockages come from leaves, bird nests or ice and debris. A plumber can clear a vent through the roof or find a collapsed section, and the job is not safe for a homeowner on a steep roof.

When the smell comes from the sewer line

A smell that returns after you refill the traps, comes with slow drains in several fixtures or follows a backup points to the line itself. Cracks and bellies allow gas to escape and waste to collect.

A camera inspection shows the cause. If a section is cracked, sewer line repair is the fix, and if the line is coated with buildup, hydro jetting cleans it. Our sewer inspection page describes what the camera shows.

Toilet seals and floor drains

A toilet that rocks even slightly can break its wax seal and let gas escape, often with no water leak. Tighten the closet bolts evenly, and if the smell remains, the toilet needs to be reset on a new seal.

Floor drains in laundry rooms and garages can also hold old residue that smells when warm. Pour in hot water and a mild cleaner, then refill the trap. If the smell returns quickly, the drain line itself may be partly blocked.

Outdoor smells near the yard

A sewer smell outdoors near a cleanout or a green wet strip of lawn suggests a break in the lateral. Check the cleanout cap too, since a loose cap releases gas right at the surface.

If you also smell it in the house after rain or irrigation, groundwater may be entering a cracked pipe. That is a job for a camera inspection, and a camera run can often be done in a single visit.

Keeping smells away

  • Run water in rarely used drains every few weeks.
  • Keep grease and wipes out of drains.
  • Make sure floor drains have water, or fit a trap primer.
  • Schedule a camera look if the house is old or a tree grows near the line.

Traps, vents and the code

The California Plumbing Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), requires a trap and a vent on every fixture drain so that sewer gas stays out of the house. A smell usually means one of those two parts has failed or dried out.
